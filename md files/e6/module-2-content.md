# **Module 2a: Feeling Good**

## **1. Reading Passage**

### **Trying to Share Our "Epic" Moments May Leave Us Feeling Left Out**

**[A]** We love to reminisce and tell others about our extraordinary experiences – that time we climbed Mount Kilimanjaro, got to taste that rare food or ran into a celebrity on the street – but new research suggests this may not be such a wise idea [222]. In fact, the findings, published in the latest edition of a psychology journal, suggest that sharing these extraordinary experiences are not as beneficial as we might think and can actually come at a social cost [222].

**[B]** "Extraordinary experiences are pleasurable in the moment but can leave us socially worse off in the long run," says psychological scientist and study author Gus Cooney of Harvard University [222].

**[C]** "The participants in our study mistakenly thought that having an extraordinary experience would make them the star of the conversation [222]. But they were wrong, because to be extraordinary is to be different than other people, and [222]

**[D]** social interaction is grounded in similarities [222]."

Cooney, who conducted the research with co-authors Daniel T. Gilbert of Harvard University and Timothy D. Wilson of the University of Virginia, was interested in exploring the negative consequences of extraordinary experiences based on his own observations with others [223]. He had noticed that we are keen to tell our friends about new or rare experiences, but most successful conversations are about ordinary topics [223]. He therefore wondered "if there might be times when extraordinary experiences have more costs than benefits, and whether people know what those times are [223]."

To find out if he was right, Cooney, Gilbert and Wilson invited sixty-eight participants to their laboratory in groups of four [224]. In each group, one participant was asked to watch a highly rated video of a street magician performing for a crowd, while the other three participants were asked to watch a lower-rated animated video [224]. Participants were told about each video’s rating [224]. After watching the videos, the participants sat around a table and had a five-minute unstructured conversation about them [224].

The findings were not what participants expected [225]. It seems we should be more careful when choosing and sharing our experiences, because the participants who watched the higher-rated video, the "extraordinary experiencers", reported feeling worse after the group discussion than those who watched the lower-rated video [225]. Even though, before the experiment, all participants thought that the "extraordinary experiencer" would talk more during the post-video discussion, that person actually spoke less and felt excluded from the conversation as a result [225].

---

### **Critical Response Passage: "Feeling Good"**

There are two main reasons why you might not agree with Cooney, Gilbert and Wilson’s research conclusions, and these do not include the fact that sixty-eight people is not a very high number to include in a study [227]. 

Firstly, the video of a street magician is not the same as an incredible life experience [227]. The research uses cost-benefit analysis, i.e. the benefit of watching the video minus the cost of not being included socially [227]. But it is unlikely that a street performer, even an amazing one, produces results similar to the feeling of an extraordinary experience [227]. So, when the cost of not being included is taken away from the benefit of watching the video, of course the result will be more negative [227].

Secondly, one wonders why the researchers assume that people who do extraordinary things only spend time with boring people who have no bucket list themselves [228]? In reality, if you explore the Costa Rican rainforest, maybe your friend goes sailing, gets an amazing professional qualification, or watches his child graduate from college [228]. All of these can be considered great topics of conversation [228]. Of course people will always have lots of everyday experiences to talk about too [228]. After all, when you return from an inspiring trip, you still have to do the laundry and call your parents [228].

## **3. Module 2a Language Development: Relative Clauses**

### **A. Core Grammar Rules [980, 981]**
1.  **Defining Relative Clauses:** Used to identify, classify, or define a noun/pronoun [127, 980].
    *   *Example:* "Happiness is an emotion **which** makes us feel good [127, 980]."
2.  **Relative Pronouns as Subject:** Cannot be followed by a subject pronoun (*he, she, it*, etc.) [127, 980].
    *   *Correct:* "A person **who** is stressed has high blood pressure."
    *   *Incorrect:* "A person who is stressed he has high blood pressure [981]."
3.  **Relative Pronouns as Object:** Can be omitted when they act as the object of the relative clause [981].
    *   *Example:* "Embarrassment is a feeling **(that)** we don’t want to experience [981]."
4.  **Key Relative Pronouns [981]:**
    *   *which/that* – things [981]
    *   *who/that* – people [981]
    *   *whose* – possessions [981]
    *   *where* (places) / *when* (time) act similarly to relative clauses [981, 982].

## **4. Module 2a Writing (Task 2): Opinion Essay**

### **Task Prompt:**
> *Our relationships with work colleagues or classmates are as important to our happiness as our relationship with our family [424, 599].*
> *To what extent do you agree or disagree [424, 599]?*

### **Official Model Answer (Band 6+) [425, 426]:**
The statement argues that relationships with our colleagues or fellow students have as much impact on our happiness as relationships with our own family [425]. My view is that both types of relationship are extremely important but that, ultimately, family relationships are more significant [425].

I think that family relationships are key to our happiness because family ties are unbreakable [425]. My sister, for example, moved to the other side of the world and has lost touch with friends and former colleagues but she has always stayed in contact with family [425]. Moreover, our families support us whatever we do [425]. They help us when times are difficult and take genuine pleasure in our successes [425]. The people we work or study with may not know what to do when we have a serious problem and so just stay away [425]. If we win a prize or gain a promotion, they may feel more envious than happy about our good fortune [425].

This does not mean that I believe that relationships with friends and colleagues are unimportant [426]. Quite the opposite is true [426]. In some ways, we may have more in common with people who work or study in the same place [426]. We often, after all, have very different interests and tastes from our parents or even from our brothers and sisters [426]. In contrast, our colleagues and fellow students tend to have similar goals in life to our own [426]. We share many acquaintances with them and often spend leisure time together, enjoying some very special times together [426].

In conclusion, I think that all relationships are important to people’s happiness [427]. We need friends and colleagues as well as family [427]. But, as the saying goes, blood is thicker than water, and family relationships in the end matter more when it comes to living a happy life [427].

# **Module 2b: Looking Good**

## **1. Reading Passage**

### **Clothes and Identity: We Are What We Wear**

**[A]** We all communicate through fashion [746]. Our clothes, accessories, hairstyles and make-up are all things we use to promote ourselves to others [746]. Fashion is about meanings and symbols that give us immediate visual communication [746]. But I would suggest it is also about decision-making, performance and fitting into society [746].

**[B]** It is certainly true that fashion is linked to how we view each other [747]. We make decisions based on a person’s appearance within a second [747]. Clothing style gives information about a person’s character, sociability and intelligence [747]. Attractive people generate a "halo effect", which makes us believe everything connected with that person is positive [747]. Consequently, we often think attractive people are more intelligent, healthy and sociable compared with unattractive people [747]. In a recent study supported by a tailoring company, participants were shown images of a man for three seconds [747]. The first image showed him in a suit made especially for him [747]. The second showed him in a suit bought from a high-street shop [747]. People thought the man in the first image was more confident, successful and a higher earner than in the second [747].

**[C]** Researchers have also looked at the difference between wanting what we have and having what we want, and they made an interesting discovery [748]. They found that we get more pleasure from buying objects than we do from owning the objects themselves [748]. We enjoy shopping because it allows us to compare ourselves with others [748]. Our new Prada shoes may be lovely, but our pleasure in them depends on impressing others [748]. You’d think that buyer’s guilt would stop us shopping but it doesn’t [748]. In fact, psychologists found that feeling guilty often pushes us to shop more [748]. And when we can’t find what we want, we are motivated to try even harder because our brains release the chemical dopamine which gives us pleasure [748]. The longer it takes us to find what we want, the more dopamine our brain releases [748].

**[D]** The shopping experience is not always the same for both genders, however [749]. From a young age, women are judged on their appearance, and men on skills and abilities [749]. Fashion can be used to change appearance, self-identity and boost confidence by showing some qualities and hiding others [749]. It is therefore not surprising that shopping is more popular among women [749]. It is beginning to change but in a 2013 paper, researchers found 80 percent of men hate shopping with their partner, and 45 percent try not to go at all costs [749]. Half of couples fight when they shop together, as men claim they get bored, hungry or thirsty and wish they were outside [749]. If we compare this to the past when men hunted alone, as fast as possible, and women spent time with each other selecting berries, nuts and vegetables, we can see shopping is the same [749]. Women take their time and do it with friends [749]. Men prefer to shop alone and fast [749].

**[E]** Clothes and accessories are not only connected with our identity; they also affect how we feel and perform [750]. When we give meaning to the clothes we wear, we can improve our cognitive abilities, which can influence our social interactions [750]. Researchers found that simply wearing a lab coat increased performance on attention-related tasks [750]. And, more interestingly, when it was described as a doctor’s coat as opposed to a painter’s, sustained attention increased even more [750]. Psychologists call this effect "enclothed cognition" [750].

**[F]** Finally, fashion can allow us to fit into a group or stand out as different [751]. Groups have a "uniform" that identifies membership (e.g., punk, City worker) [751]. Our desire to be part of the group will influence the way we dress, and following the latest trends can give us membership of the "fashionable" group [751]. This is a problem for fashion, as people think it is about looking different [751]. But once an item becomes fashionable, it is no longer different [751]. Uniforms are frequently adopted by fashion and other industry leaders [751]. For example, fashion magazine editor Anna Wintour only wears printed sleeveless dresses, and famous model Kate Moss is addicted to skinny jeans and ankle boots [751]. Wearing the same clothes every day means you do not have to worry about what to wear and people immediately recognise you [751].
