# Module 2a: Feeling Good

## 1. Reading Passages

### Trying to Share Our 'Epic' Moments May Leave Us Feeling Left Out

**[A]** We love to reminisce and tell others about our extraordinary experiences – that time we climbed Mount Kilimanjaro, got to taste that rare food or ran into a celebrity on the street – but new research suggests this may not be such a wise idea. In fact, the findings, published in the latest edition of a psychology journal, suggest that sharing these extraordinary experiences are not as beneficial as we might think and can actually come at a social cost.

**[B]** "Extraordinary experiences are pleasurable in the moment but can leave us socially worse off in the long run," says psychological scientist and study author Gus Cooney of Harvard University.

**[C]** "The participants in our study mistakenly thought that having an extraordinary experience would make them the star of the conversation. But they were wrong, because to be extraordinary is to be different than other people, and 

**[D]** social interaction is grounded in similarities."

Cooney, who conducted the research with co-authors Daniel T. Gilbert of Harvard University and Timothy D. Wilson of the University of Virginia, was interested in exploring the negative consequences of extraordinary experiences based on his own observations with others. He had noticed that we are keen to tell our friends about new or rare experiences, but most successful conversations are about ordinary topics. He therefore wondered "if there might be times when extraordinary experiences have more costs than benefits, and whether people know what those times are."

To find out if he was right, Cooney, Gilbert and Wilson invited sixty-eight participants to their laboratory in groups of four. In each group, one participant was asked to watch a highly rated video of a street magician performing for a crowd, while the other three participants were asked to watch a lower-rated animated video. Participants were told about each video’s rating. After watching the videos, the participants sat around a table and had a five-minute unstructured conversation about them.

The findings were not what participants expected. It seems we should be more careful when choosing and sharing our experiences, because the participants who watched the higher-rated video, the "extraordinary experiencers", reported feeling worse after the group discussion than those who watched the lower-rated video. Even though, before the experiment, all participants thought that the "extraordinary experiencer" would talk more during the post-video discussion, that person actually spoke less and felt excluded from the conversation as a result.

---

### Critical Response Passage: "Feeling Good"

There are two main reasons why you might not agree with Cooney, Gilbert and Wilson’s research conclusions, and these do not include the fact that sixty-eight people is not a very high number to include in a study.

Firstly, the video of a street magician is not the same as an incredible life experience. The research uses cost-benefit analysis, i.e. the benefit of watching the video minus the cost of not being included socially. But it is unlikely that a street performer, even an amazing one, produces results similar to the feeling of an extraordinary experience. So, when the cost of not being included is taken away from the benefit of watching the video, of course the result will be more negative.

Secondly, one wonders why the researchers assume that people who do extraordinary things only spend time with boring people who have no bucket list themselves? In reality, if you explore the Costa Rican rainforest, maybe your friend goes sailing, gets an amazing professional qualification, or watches his child graduate from college. All of these can be considered great topics of conversation. Of course people will always have lots of everyday experiences to talk about too. After all, when you return from an inspiring trip, you still have to do the laundry and call your parents.

## 3. Module 2a Language Development: Relative Clauses

### A. Core Grammar Rules
1.  **Defining Relative Clauses:** We use defining relative clauses to identify or classify a noun or pronoun.
    *   *Example:* "Happiness is an emotion **which** makes us feel good."
2.  **Relative Pronouns as Subject:** We use a relative pronoun as the subject of a relative clause. We do **NOT** use a subject pronoun (*he, she, it*, etc.) after a subject relative pronoun.
    *   *Correct:* "A person **who** is stressed has high blood pressure."
    *   *Incorrect:* "A person who is stressed he has high blood pressure."
3.  **Relative Pronouns as Object:** We use a relative pronoun as the object of a relative clause. We can **omit** the relative pronoun if it acts as the object.
    *   *Example:* "Embarrassment is a feeling **(that)** we don’t want to experience."
4.  **Relative Pronouns Usage:**
    *   *which / that* – things
    *   *who / that* – people
    *   *whose* – possessions
    *   *where* (places) and *when* (time) act in a similar way to relative clauses.
5.  **Formality Note:** In defining relative clauses, *that* can replace *which* and *who*. In more formal and academic texts, *which* and *who* are preferred.

## 4. Module 2a Writing (Task 2): Opinion Essay

### Task Prompt:
> *Our relationships with work colleagues or classmates are as important to our happiness as our relationship with our family.*
> *To what extent do you agree or disagree?*

### Detailed Essay Plan:
*   **Introduction:** Paraphrase the prompt. State a clear opinion (both are crucial, but family is ultimately more significant).
*   **Body 1 (The Significance of Family):** Highlight that family ties are unbreakable, lifelong, and offer unconditional support.
*   **Body 2 (The Role of Colleagues & Classmates):** Contrast this by discussing common interests, shared day-to-day goals, and leisure time, while noting potential rivalry.
*   **Conclusion:** Reiterate that both spheres are necessary for a balanced, happy life, but family is the bedrock ("blood is thicker than water").

### Official Model Answer (Band 6+):
The statement argues that relationships with our colleagues or fellow students have as much impact on our happiness as relationships with our own family. My view is that both types of relationship are extremely important but that, ultimately, family relationships are more significant.

I think that family relationships are key to our happiness because family ties are unbreakable. My sister, for example, moved to the other side of the world and has lost touch with friends and former colleagues but she has always stayed in contact with family. Moreover, our families support us whatever we do. They help us when times are difficult and take genuine pleasure in our successes. The people we work or study with may not know what to do when we have a serious problem and so just stay away. If we win a prize or gain a promotion, they may feel more envious than happy about our good fortune.

This does not mean that I believe that relationships with friends and colleagues are unimportant. Quite the opposite is true. In some ways, we may have more in common with people who work or study in the same place. We often, after all, have very different interests and tastes from our parents or even from our brothers and sisters. In contrast, our colleagues and fellow students tend to have similar goals in life to our own. We share many acquaintances with them and often spend leisure time together, enjoying some very special times together.

In conclusion, I think that all relationships are important to people’s happiness. We need friends and colleagues as well as family. But, as the saying goes, blood is thicker than water, and family relationships in the end matter more when it comes to living a happy life.

# Module 2b: Looking Good

## 1. Reading Passage

### Clothes and Identity: We Are What We Wear

**[A]** We all communicate through fashion. Our clothes, accessories, hairstyles and make-up are all things we use to promote ourselves to others. Fashion is about meanings and symbols that give us immediate visual communication. But I would suggest it is also about decision-making, performance and fitting into society.

**[B]** It is certainly true that fashion is linked to how we view each other. We make decisions based on a person’s appearance within a second. Clothing style gives information about a person’s character, sociability and intelligence. Attractive people generate a "halo effect", which makes us believe everything connected with that person is positive. Consequently, we often think attractive people are more intelligent, healthy and sociable compared with unattractive people. In a recent study supported by a tailoring company, participants were shown images of a man for three seconds. The first image showed him in a suit made especially for him. The second showed him in a suit bought from a high-street shop. People thought the man in the first image was more confident, successful and a higher earner than in the second.

**[C]** Researchers have also looked at the difference between wanting what we have and having what we want, and they made an interesting discovery. They found that we get more pleasure from buying objects than we do from owning the objects themselves. We enjoy shopping because it allows us to compare ourselves with others. Our new Prada shoes may be lovely, but our pleasure in them depends on impressing others. You’d think that buyer’s guilt would stop us shopping but it doesn’t. In fact, psychologists found that feeling guilty often pushes us to shop more. And when we can’t find what we want, we are motivated to try even harder because our brains release the chemical dopamine which gives us pleasure. The longer it takes us to find what we want, the more dopamine our brain releases.

**[D]** The shopping experience is not always the same for both genders, however. From a young age, women are judged on their appearance, and men on skills and abilities. Fashion can be used to change appearance, self-identity and boost confidence by showing some qualities and hiding others. It is therefore not surprising that shopping is more popular among women. It is beginning to change but in a 2013 paper, researchers found 80 percent of men hate shopping with their partner, and 45 percent try not to go at all costs. Half of couples fight when they shop together, as men claim they get bored, hungry or thirsty and wish they were outside. If we compare this to the past when men hunted alone, as fast as possible, and women spent time with each other selecting berries, nuts and vegetables, we can see shopping is the same. Women take their time and do it with friends. Men prefer to shop alone and fast.

**[E]** Clothes and accessories are not only connected with our identity; they also affect how we feel and perform. When we give meaning to the clothes we wear, we can improve our cognitive abilities, which can influence our social interactions. Researchers found that simply wearing a lab coat increased performance on attention-related tasks. And, more interestingly, when it was described as a doctor’s coat as opposed to a painter’s, sustained attention increased even more. Psychologists call this effect "enclothed cognition".

**[F]** Finally, fashion can allow us to fit into a group or stand out as different. Groups have a "uniform" that identifies membership (e.g., punk, City worker). Our desire to be part of the group will influence the way we dress, and following the latest trends can give us membership of the "fashionable" group. This is a problem for fashion, as people think it is about looking different. But once an item becomes fashionable, it is no longer different. Uniforms are frequently adopted by fashion and other industry leaders. For example, fashion magazine editor Anna Wintour only wears printed sleeveless dresses, and famous model Kate Moss is addicted to skinny jeans and ankle boots. Wearing the same clothes every day means you do not have to worry about what to wear and people immediately recognise you.

### B. Parts of Speech Sentence Analysis
*To master academic writing, candidates must recognize how different parts of speech function in a sentence.*

> **Target Sentence:** *"Half the participants made themselves look big for two minutes before a very difficult job interview but the other half didn’t."*

1.  **Noun** (*person, place, concept, or thing*): **participants, minutes, interview**.
2.  **Verb** (*action, event, or state*): **made, look**.
3.  **Auxiliary Verb** (*helps the main verb*): **didn’t**.
4.  **Adjective** (*describes a noun*): **big, difficult**.
5.  **Adverb** (*gives more information about a verb, adjective, or clause*): **very**.
6.  **Preposition** (*connects nouns/pronouns to other elements*): **for, before**.
7.  **Pronoun** (*replaces a noun*): **themselves**.
8.  **Article** (*determiner preceding a noun*): **the, a**.
9.  **Conjunction** (*connects clauses or sentences*): **but**.

#### Rules of Sentence Grammar:
1.  Sentences usually start with a **pronoun** or noun phrase.
2.  Articles come before a **noun**.
3.  Adjectives describe a **noun** (placed before it or after a linking verb).
4.  Adverbs come before or after a **verb** or adjective.
5.  Prepositions usually come before a **noun or number**.
6.  A conjunction joins two **clauses or sentences**.
7.  Auxiliary verbs come before **main verbs**.

#### Grammar of Intensifiers:
1.  **Regular Adjectives:** modified by *very, really, extremely, incredibly* (e.g., *very anxious, extremely proud*).
2.  **Extreme Adjectives:** modified by **absolutely** (e.g., *absolutely furious, absolutely terrified*). They cannot be modified by *very* or *quite*.

        *   Angry $\rightarrow$ **Furious**
        *   Tired $\rightarrow$ **Exhausted**
        *   Scared $\rightarrow$ **Terrified**
        *   Bad $\rightarrow$ **Terrible**
3.  **Versatile Modifier:** **really** can be used grammatically with both regular and extreme adjectives (e.g., *really angry, really furious*).

### Official Model Answer (Band 6+):
For centuries, people have taken care of the way they dress. However, traditionally it has been women who are more concerned with their appearance than men. This essay will argue that women’s attitudes to appearance have not changed in recent decades, whereas men have become more concerned with how they look.

Men, however, are required to pay much more attention to their appearance these days so they have good skin and look as good as celebrity role models. Appearance is now equally important for both men and women.

## 5. IELTS Speaking Practice: Feelings, Experiences & Fashion

### Part 1: Quick-Fire Topics
1.  **Do you find it easy to talk about your feelings with other people?**
    *   *Band 7.5+ Model Answer:* Generally, it depends on who I'm with. With close friends and immediate family, I'm quite open and expressive. However, in professional or academic contexts, I prefer to remain composed and keep my personal emotions private.
2.  **What kind of clothes do you feel most comfortable wearing?**
    *   *Band 7.5+ Model Answer:* I definitely gravitate towards smart-casual outfits. A well-fitted pair of jeans with a breathable cotton shirt or casual blazer makes me feel relaxed yet presentable, whether I'm at university or meeting friends.
3.  **Do you enjoy sharing your travel experiences on social media?**
    *   *Band 7.5+ Model Answer:* Occasionally, yes, but I try to be mindful. As recent psychological studies suggest, boasting about extraordinary trips can sometimes alienate people, so I prefer sharing practical tips or funny everyday moments rather than showing off.

### Part 2: Long Turn (Cue Card)
> **Describe an extraordinary experience you shared with someone.**
> 
> You should say:
> * What the experience was
> * Who you were with
> * What happened during the event
> * And explain how you felt after sharing this experience with them.

*Band 8.0 Model Response:*
> "I’d like to talk about the time my brother and I went night-kayaking in a bioluminescent bay in Puerto Rico. It was about two years ago during a summer break. The water was filled with microscopic organisms that glowed neon blue whenever our paddles disturbed the surface.
> 
> What made it truly memorable was that neither of us had ever witnessed anything like it before. Gliding through pitch-black water under a starry sky while every paddle stroke lit up like lightning was absolutely breathtaking. Afterwards, sitting on the beach drinking hot cocoa, we felt an immense sense of wonder and shared joy. Sharing that rare spectacle strengthened our bond, and it remains one of our favorite shared memories."

### Part 3: In-Depth Discussion
1.  **How does a person's appearance affect the way other people judge them in job interviews?**
    *   *Band 8.0+ Model Answer:* Appearance plays a profound role due to the psychological phenomenon known as the 'halo effect'. When an applicant is smartly dressed and well-groomed, interviewers subconsciously attribute competence, confidence, and diligence to them within the first few seconds. This initial cognitive bias can significantly sway the outcome of an interview.
2.  **Do you think social media has made people more obsessed with looking good?**
    *   *Band 7.5+ Model Answer:* Undoubtedly. Platforms driven by visual content create continuous peer comparison. People are bombarded with curated, filtered images of beauty and luxury, which often triggers anxiety and buyer's guilt, pushing individuals to invest excessively in fast fashion and cosmetic products.
