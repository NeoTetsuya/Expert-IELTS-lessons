## Module 9a: The News

### 1. Reading: Understand Connections & Matching Features
In the IELTS Reading test, matching features tasks require you to associate specific statements or characteristics with groups of people, organisations, or time periods. These statements are paraphrased, so you must recognize synonyms and parallel concepts.

#### Reading Passage: "Changing habits in accessing the news"

**[Paragraph 1]**
There are many different ways to get the news nowadays [144]. Some people buy a daily newspaper, others watch television or listen to the radio and still others choose to get their news online [144]. Many, of course, make regular use of more than one source [144].

**[Paragraph 2]**
The Pew Research Center in the US recently conducted a survey with 3,612 adults nationwide [145]. Researchers wanted to find out how they currently learn about the news [145]. They ended up classifying the people they interviewed into four groups: Traditionalists, Net-newsers, Integrators and the Disengaged [145].

**[Paragraph 3]**
The researchers called the largest group Traditionalists [145]. As the name suggests, they prefer to access the news in a traditional way, typically by watching television [145]. They do this a great deal – in the morning, in the afternoon and in the evening [145]. They explain that they understand the news better when they see pictures rather than just reading or listening to a news story [145]. Their level of education is relatively low, with over 60 percent of this group having no more than a high school education [145]. This group tends to be middle-aged or older, and is less well-paid than people who access the news in other ways [145]. They say they like to get information about the weather but do not find stories about science or technology interesting [145]. Most people in this group have a computer at home but they almost never use it to find out about the news [145]. Although they are still a large group – 46 percent of the population – their numbers have been going down for ten years now [145].

**[Paragraph 4]**
Net-newsers, on the other hand, choose to get the majority of their news from the internet [146]. Sometimes they read online news articles or blogs and sometimes they watch video clips of news stories [146]. Their online access to the news is highest during the day [146]. They use technology in other aspects of their lives too and like to follow stories relating to science [146]. The smallest of the four groups, they are also the youngest, with a typical age of 35 [146]. They are better educated than other groups; eight in ten of them have at least attended college [146, 147]. Nearly 60 percent of the group are men and they also tend to be relatively well-off [147]. Although the internet is by far the main source of news for Net-newsers, they do occasionally use other sources too [147]. They are, for example, at least as likely as Integrators and Traditionalists to read serious news magazines [147].

**[Paragraph 5]**
The third group are the Integrators [147]. These are people who regularly use both television and the internet as their source of news [147]. They usually watch the news on television in the evenings and log on to the internet from work in order to keep up with the news during the day [147]. Almost half of them also get some of their news from the radio [147]. Integrators are people with a particularly strong interest in the news and spend more time following it than any of the other groups [147]. They keep up-to-date with national and international politics and they also follow sports news [147]. They currently represent 23 percent of the population but this proportion is growing steadily [147]. They typically have higher education and a high salary [147]. Most of them are middle-aged [147].

**[Paragraph 6]**
The researchers reported that 14 percent of the US population fall into the fourth and final group, which they called the Disengaged [148]. This group has little or no interest in the news [148]. Almost half of the group do not watch the television news even once a day and one-fifth do not know which party is in power in Washington [148]. In general, they have even fewer academic qualifications than the Traditionalists [148]. There are signs that the proportion of young people in the Disengaged category is rising [148]. Slightly over one-third of the under-25 age group reported that on a typical day, they read or see no news, up from one quarter in 1998 [148].

**[Paragraph 7]**
The Pew Center’s research study highlights interesting trends in the ways people access news today [149]. One of these is the fact that there are increasing extremes of behaviour in the US population [149]. While there are many people who are accessing news stories much more frequently and in a variety of different ways, the number of people who pay no attention at all to the news is also on the increase [149].

### 2. Language Development: Present Perfect and Past Simple
Understanding when to use the Present Perfect vs. the Past Simple is essential for IELTS.
*   We use the **Past Simple** for actions completed at a definite time in the past [150, 185]. It is often accompanied by time markers such as *yesterday, last year, when [I got my tablet], ago* [150].
*   We use the **Present Perfect** for actions connected to the present, or where the exact time is unspecified or unimportant [150, 189]. It is used with markers like *already, ever, never, since, still, yet, lately* [150].
*   **For** can be used with both tenses (*for five years* can describe a completed or unfinished situation) [150, 189].

### 4. Writing: Task 1 Comparing and Contrasting Data (Pie Charts)
In IELTS Writing Task 1, you must select and report the main features of charts, making comparisons where relevant. When comparing two age groups, utilize contrastive transition markers like *while, in contrast, although, however*, and *in comparison with* [164, 177, 279].

#### Verbatim Writing Task
*The pie charts below show the responses of people in two different age groups (16–24 and 55+) across 26 countries to a survey asking: "What is your main source of news?" [163]*

#### Source Distribution Data [163]

| Source of News | People Aged 16–24 | People Aged 55+ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Online (including social media)** | 64% | 25% |
| **Television (TV)** | 25% | 55% |
| **Print (newspapers and magazines)** | 6% | 12% |
| **Radio** | 5% | 8% |

#### Verbatim IELTS Model Answer (Pie Charts)
> **The charts illustrate the principal source of news for young adults (aged 16–24) and for people over 55. They summarise information gathered from 26 European, Asian and North American countries [163].**
> 
> **The charts show that the two age groups access news in rather different ways [164]. A majority of the younger people get their news online, while the older generation, in contrast, choose television as their main source [164].**
> 
> **Although use of TV and the internet differ for these two age groups, the radio is the least important source of information for both groups [164]. A similarly small proportion of each group – 5 percent and 8 percent – name the radio as their main source of news [164].**
> 
> **Printed newspapers and magazines, the most traditional way of accessing news, still has some importance for both the age groups illustrated in the charts [164]. However, they are more popular with older people: 12 percent of the over-55s name this as their main source of information, in comparison with only 6 percent of 16- to 24-year-olds [164].**

### 5. Speaking: Expressing Attitude in Part 3 Discussions
Use attitude adverbs to signal your opinion during speaking tasks [160, 201].

*   **100% Certain/Evident**: *clearly, naturally, obviously, without a doubt* [201, 202]
*   **Reported / Heard**: *apparently, reportedly* [202]
*   **Emotional Evaluation**: *sadly, worryingly, fortunately* [201, 202]

#### Speaking Model Transcript (Part 3)
**Examiner**: *How has the way we get news changed in the last few years?* [201]

**Candidate**: "Well, **clearly**, it has changed a lot [201]. In the past people got news from traditional media like print newspapers, radio and TV and you usually got news once or twice a day [201]. But now we have constant updates on the internet and on social media, so **naturally**, everyone expects to get news quickly and news soon becomes old [201]. As a result, I don’t think people read about the news in as much detail as they did in the past [201]. **Sadly**, it’s also easier now to get inaccurate news because people get a lot of information from social media, which is usually less reliable than traditional media [201]."

**Examiner**: *And how easy is it to control the stories that the media publish?* [202]

**Candidate**: "Well, this is a difficult question because it depends on the type of media [202]. I know some famous people have tried to stop newspapers or magazines publishing stories about them but **apparently**, people on social media were discussing the same stories [202]. So I think it’s easier to control stories on traditional media, but **obviously**, it’s more difficult to control social media [202]. People can say anything they like on social media, whether it is true or not [202]."

## Module 9b: Technology

### 1. Reading: Matching Features & Sentence Completion

#### Reading Passage: "Brain health and technology"

**[Paragraph A]**
We live in the digital age but some people say that too much use of technology will have a negative effect on our psychology and mental abilities [297]. According to them, if school children use, for example, calculators instead of working out a sum in their head, they will not be able to understand basic arithmetic [297]. Similarly, adults will become too lazy to check their spelling if they can rely on a spell-check program [297]. In other words, if computers do the work for people, they may not learn to think for themselves [297]. However, a number of research studies have shown that technology does not necessarily limit mental abilities [297]. It seems that it can, in fact, actually have a positive impact on them [297].

**[Paragraph B]**
The brain is extremely important when it comes to being healthy and living well [298]. Exercise makes more blood flow to the brain, so it is one of the simplest ways to get the brain working better [298]. It also improves the chemicals which protect nerves from damage [298]. However, exercise must be energetic enough to be effective [298]. People can use technology like heart monitors to check that they are exercising at the right level [298]. Another way to use technology to make exercise more effective is to use devices with a GPS (Global Positioning System) [298]. This allows people to know, for example, how great a distance they have run and to plan future runs in their area [298].

**[Paragraph C]**
Music is known to have a positive impact on feelings of happiness and well-being [299]. In addition, there is a theory, called the Mozart Effect, that listening to classical music makes a person cleverer [299]. Experiments have indeed shown that listening to music has improved language ability in patients with certain illnesses [299]. Electronic devices now make it possible to listen to music on public transport, at work, while exercising or at any other time during the day [299]. So this is another way in which digital devices may be making people’s mental abilities stronger [299].

**[Paragraph D]**
Changes in diet can also help to improve brain function [300]. Omega-3 fats, for instance, are good for the brain and provide important support to the nervous system [300]. Similarly, research shows that having coconut oil in the diet is especially helpful for the brain and can help improve memory [300]. Vitamin D is also good as it improves nerve growth in the brain [300]. Technology provides a quick and easy way for people to check what nutrients there are in specific foods [300]. Having this information easily available on a smartphone app is very convenient when shoppers are selecting food [300]. They can choose products based on accurate information rather than just guessing what a particular food contains [300].

**[Paragraph E]**
Many people use their electronic devices to help them stay in contact with others [301]. And having a network of social relationships is also important in keeping the brain working well [301]. Research shows this to be a key factor in keeping older people happy [301]. Social media sites, texting and video calls all make staying in touch with one another so much easier than it used to be, even if they live on different continents [301]. Moreover, the internet means it is possible for people who share the same interests to make contact with each other [301].

**[Paragraph F]**
Another way for people to improve their brain function is by exercising it with challenging mental tasks [302]. Psychologists have shown that learning new things actually changes the size and structure of brain neurons and develops more connections between them [302]. Smartphones, tablets and computers make it very easy for people to have access to information and tasks that are both fun and stimulating [302]. For instance, the internet offers all sorts of amazing opportunities for study [302]. Research has shown that surfing the web increases activity in the parts of the brain used for decision-making [302].

**[Paragraph G]**
To sum up, despite frequent criticisms of the impact of technology on people today, electronic devices can have a very positive impact on brain health and mental powers [303]. They can help people exercise, listen to music, diet, stay in contact with friends and find mental challenges [303]. All these activities play a very valuable role in improving not only health and happiness but also mental ability [303].

### 5. Speaking: Asking for Clarification and Repetition in Part 3
In IELTS Speaking Part 3, if you do not understand a term or a question, ask for clarification or repetition. This maintains your fluency score [311].

#### Standard Phrases to Memorize [310]:
1.  **Clarifying terms**: *"When you say ‘technology’, do you mean computers and mobile phones?"* [310]
2.  **Requesting repetition**: *"Could you repeat the question, please?"* [310]
3.  **Unfamiliar words**: *"Sorry, I’m not sure what ‘gadget’ means. Could you explain it in another way, please?"* [310]
4.  **Clarifying abstract nouns**: *"Could you please explain what ‘rate’ means?"* [310]
5.  **Expressing non-understanding**: *"Sorry, I don’t understand the question. Could you explain?"* [310]
6.  **Polite requests**: *"Sorry, would you mind repeating the question?"* [311]
