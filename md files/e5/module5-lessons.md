#### Reading Passage: "A different way to see the world"
*Mark Beaumont found that many countries look different from a bicycle. Our reporter writes about Mark’s experience of cycling round the world [114].*

**[Paragraph A]**
An extraordinary cyclist, Mark Beaumont, has completed a number of amazing long-distance trips on his bike [110]. In 2008, for example, he rode for 18,296 miles around the world [110]. Two years later he made a 13,080-mile journey through the Americas [110]. In 2015 he cycled 6,762 miles from Cairo to Cape Town [110].

**[Paragraph B]**
On that last journey Mark succeeded in breaking – by as much as 17 days – the world record for cycling across Africa [111]. To do this, he rode 160 miles a day [111]. He explains that it was certainly not easy to keep up this speed [111]. There were, for example, long delays at border crossings and queues of traffic often stopped him from travelling fast [111]. Broken roads caused problems and the weather also affected how fast he could go [111].

**[Paragraph C]**
On all his journeys Mark has been interested by the landscapes he has seen [111]. He says these often differ from what he expected [111]. ‘When people think about Patagonia, for instance, they imagine the beautiful mountains and lakes you see in holiday brochures; but when I cycled there, I realised that large parts of it are just miles and miles of grassland [111]. What is romantic and exciting for most holidaymakers can become very boring when you see nothing else for several days [111].’

**[Paragraph D]**
Of all the landscapes he has seen, Mark has particularly enjoyed some of the wild parts of Texas and the Deep South of the USA [112]. ‘I remember waking before daylight [112]. I had to remove the ice from my tent when it was still dark [112]. Then I set out on roads with absolutely no one else around [112]. I was in the middle of this enormous desert and all I could hear was the sound of animals calling [112]. When I look back now, it was one of my favourite times: to be totally alone in that environment [112].’

**[Paragraph E]**
His travels have also taught Mark about how differently people around the world view cycling [112]. In Europe, North America and Australia, cycling is a sport which people do mainly to keep fit and have fun [112]. But in many places Mark visited, a bicycle is something rather different [112]. It is a type of transport that many people dream of getting one day [112]. People who are used to walking everywhere wish they had enough money to buy a bike in order to get from A to B much more quickly and easily [112]. People who see a bicycle that way often found it hard to understand why Mark wanted to cycle such long distances for no obvious reason [112].

**[Paragraph F]**
But for Mark, cycling is by far the best way to see the world [113]. ‘It’s never boring when you’re cycling [113]. You notice everything, especially when riding alone [113]. When you cycle, you get a wonderful view of the world [113]. It is amazing to see how the people and cultures change from one place to the next [113].’ The kindness of the people Mark met was what impressed him most [113]. ‘Even in the poorest countries, I never had to ask a second person for a place to stay [113].’

**[Paragraph G]**
So how does Mark feel at the end of one of his big adventures? He says that, because the projects involve huge effort, time and costs, his main emotion is relief that he has completed the trip successfully [113]. ‘It is not until later that you have time to think about what the journey meant, the landscapes you saw and the people you met [113]. And then you start thinking about where you might go next [113].’

### 2. Language Development: -ing Forms and Infinitives

Developing grammatical control over the use of **-ing forms** and **infinitives (with or without *to*)** is highly tested in IELTS [114, 154].

#### Core Grammatical Rules Matrix [154, 155]

| Structure type | Core Rules | Classroom & Exam Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **-ing Form** | 1. Used as the subject of a sentence, acting like a noun [154].<br>2. Used after prepositions [155].<br>3. Used after certain verbs (e.g. *avoid, finish, hate, enjoy, keep, like, imagine, spend time*) [155]. | - **Travelling** by plane is very expensive [155].<br>- I always feel sad **after returning** from holiday [115].<br>- I **enjoy visiting** faraway places [119].<br>- I **avoid going** there in summer [155]. |
| **Infinitive with *to*** | 1. Used to express purpose (answering *why*) [115].<br>2. Used after certain adjectives (e.g. *difficult, hard, easy*) [115].<br>3. Used after certain verbs (e.g. *decide, choose, hope, refuse, want, afford, prefer*) [114, 115]. | - I went to Egypt **to see** the pyramids [115].<br>- It can be difficult **to sleep** on a plane [115].<br>- We **decided to go** on a walking holiday [114].<br>- Some countries **refuse to let** you in without a visa [117]. |
| **Infinitive without *to*** | 1. Used after certain verbs such as *make* and *let* [115].<br>2. Used after modal verbs (e.g. *can, should, must, might*) [152, 444]. | - My parents always **made me go** camping [115].<br>- They **let us walk** round the site [117].<br>- We **might visit** the local museums [117]. |

## Module 5b: Our Environment

### 1. Reading: Visual Pollution & Matching Information

Module 5b Reading evaluates **Matching Information** across a detailed text focused on environmental and visual aesthetics [181].

#### Reading Passage: "The problem of visual pollution"

**[Paragraph A]**
Visual pollution means that people cannot enjoy what they see [332]. There are two types of visual pollution in the modern world: one is when visibility is limited by haze; another is visual untidiness, when buildings and signs spoil the view [332].

**[Paragraph B]**
Air pollution from cars and factories reduces visibility [332]. It looks like a brown haze over cities, but it also affects the countryside [332]. Scientists measured the visibility at a United States national park and found that on a clear day, it is possible to see for 320 kilometres [332]. On a hazy day, that distance falls to 48 kilometres [332].

**[Paragraph C]**
Transport and industry are both major man-made causes of air pollution [333]. But there are also natural sources of haze – smoke from forest fires, for instance [333]. The pollution that creates haze can travel thousands of kilometres [333]. In Southeast Asia, haze from enormous forest fires cost billions of dollars in health care [333]. These fires have also stopped many tourists from visiting the area in the last decade [333]. Fires in Sumatra and Borneo affected not only Indonesia, but also Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand [333]. Developers started many of these fires – often illegally – because they wanted to use the land for building or farming [333]. It is possible that some of the fires will continue to burn for years [333].

**[Paragraph D]**
Scientists can measure haze, but people have different opinions about other forms of visual pollution [334]. Wind turbines, billboards, power lines, mobile phone towers, even modern buildings can all cause different feelings [334]. To the businessman, a billboard in a good location may be beautiful [334]. But to the traveller who would like to see the hills or the pretty village behind that billboard, it is visual pollution [334].

**[Paragraph E]**
When more people started to drive in the mid-20th century, businesses put large advertising hoardings next to busy roads [334]. However, in the 1960s, many people began to complain about them, saying they were ugly and stopped drivers from focusing on the road [334]. More modern examples of visual pollution are mobile phone towers and spray-painted graffiti [334]. Some mobile phone towers have been made to look like trees or plants, which has reduced the visual impact, but attempts to ban the sale of spray paint to young people have not had much effect [334].

**[Paragraph F]**
But do these more subjective types of visual pollution really matter? The dangerous effects of air pollution are obvious, with more and more people suffering from breathing problems [335]. However, research suggests that the other types of visual pollution can also have dangerous effects [335]. One research study found that people who have an unpleasant view from their window are 40 percent more likely to feel sad or depressed [335]. And there are several studies which show that physical environment affects stress levels [335]. Being in beautiful surroundings – beside a lake or in a forest, for instance – tends to make people feel more relaxed [335].

**[Paragraph G]**
Moreover, another consequence of visual pollution is that it destroys the individual differences that make the world so special [336]. In the past every town, city and suburb had its own unique character [336]. Now they are starting to look the same all over the world [336]. There are identical fast-food restaurants, billboards, motorways and petrol stations everywhere [336]. Although this has a negative effect on the quality of life, it is probably already too late to change the situation [336].

### 3. Vocabulary: Weather, Climate Change, and Environmental Issues

Module 5b requires precise vocabulary related to natural systems, severe climate events, and human wildlife interference [181].

#### Weather Conditions & Severe Disasters [216, 217]
*   **Hurricane**: Strong, violent winds that cause a lot of damage during summer [216].
*   **Drought**: A long period of dry weather with very little or no rain [216].
*   **Flood**: A high concentration of rain, causing rivers to overflow and damage buildings [216].
*   **Heat Wave**: A long, continuous period of extremely high temperatures [216].
*   **Thunder & Lightning**: Electrical, highly noisy summer storms that light up the sky [217].
*   **Blizzard**: A severe, freezing winter snowstorm that makes travel impossible [217].
*   **Fog**: A thick, low-lying cloud that makes driving extremely dangerous [217].

#### Critical Weather Collocations [217, 218]
*   **Severe / Extreme weather** (Not *weak weather*) [217]
*   **Heavy / Strong rain** (Not *light rain* in severe contexts) [217]
*   **Tropical / Violent storm** (Not *hot storm*) [217]
*   **Freezing / Thick fog** (Not *solid fog*) [217]
*   **Mild / Extreme climate** (Not *severe climate* for regular areas) [217]
*   **High / Low temperatures** (Not *hot temperatures*) [217]

#### Collocations for Climate Change [218, 219]
1. **Global warming**: An overall, dangerous increase in world temperatures [218, 219].
2. **Polar ice cap**: The massive frozen water sheets covering the poles of the earth [219].
3. **Forest fire**: Uncontrolled, rapid burning of massive wooded or forestry areas [219].
4. **Fossil fuels**: Natural carbon sources (coal, oil, gas) burned to produce heat/power [219].
5. **Greenhouse effect**: When trapped pollution prevents solar heat from escaping the atmosphere [219].
