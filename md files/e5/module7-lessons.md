## Module 7a: Communities

### 1. Reading: Understand Sequencing & Flow Chart Completion

The primary focus of Module 7a Reading is **Flow Chart Completion** and understanding **sequencing**—tracking the exact order in which historical events or process steps occur.

#### Reading Passage: "Bournville: an unusual community"

**[Paragraph A]**
Villages, towns and cities usually grow slowly over a long period of time. However, in 19th-century Britain some places developed in a very different way. This was thanks to some very unusual men.

**[Paragraph B]**
One of the most remarkable of these was George Cadbury. He and his brother Richard had a chocolate factory in the industrial city of Birmingham. In 1879 they decided to find a new site for it and so left the city centre for a location about seven kilometres to the south. They took this decision because they thought the countryside was a healthier environment than a big city. They wanted their staff to work in a pleasant green place with fresh air. They chose a pretty location next to a little river called the Bourn.

**[Paragraph C]**
The Cadburys treated workers much better than most industrialists did at that time. They paid them good salaries and provided pensions and a full medical service. However, they wanted to do more than just provide good working conditions. In 1890 George Cadbury bought a large area of land around the factory and starting creating, at his own expense, a whole village for the factory employees. He called the village Bournville. By 1900, the village already had 314 small houses. These were traditional in design but with large gardens and modern rooms.

**[Paragraph D]**
In 1900 the Cadburys set up a trust whose purpose was to transform the village from just a collection of houses to a community. They built shops, churches, parks, sports facilities and schools so that the people who lived in Bournville had everything they could want. In order to make it look like a traditional English village, which typically has a green open space at its centre, the Trust built the public buildings around a village green. In 1906 the Trust started a housing association called Bournville Tenants Limited. This was run by the people who lived in the village, which meant they could control their own affairs. This organisation planned new houses, chose tenants and dealt with repairs to the buildings.

**[Paragraph E]**
The brothers were very keen on health and fitness and in 1920 they bought a large area of land next to the village, where they constructed football and hockey pitches, a running track, an open-air swimming pool and a fishing lake. This area also contained a large pavilion which had both changing rooms and a large hall used for balls and formal dinners. All the sports facilities were free for Cadbury staff and their families.

**[Paragraph F]**
Since then Bournville has continued to expand, with strong periods of growth in the 1950s and then again in the 1980s. At the start of the 21st century Bournville went through a £25-million redevelopment programme. The Trust pulled down all the flats from the 1950s because they wanted to build modern houses and apartments. In 2009 residents moved into the first of these modern homes, which have top-quality environmental standards and use much less energy than traditional houses.

**[Paragraph G]**
Bournville is, of course, very different today. Although the chocolate factory is still there, many of the 25,000 people who live there now work elsewhere. The number of homes in the area has grown to almost 8,000 and these vary in size from one-bedroom flats to five-bedroom houses. Bournville is now very much part of the city of Birmingham rather than a country village, but it still has good facilities and plenty of green space. And it still considers itself a distinct community.

### 2. Language Development: Zero Conditional

#### Grammatical Rules and Uses
1.  **Usage**: We use the zero conditional to discuss things that are **always or generally true**, or that always happen as a direct result of an action or situation.
2.  **Structure**: Formed using **if/when + present simple (condition clause), present simple (main clause)**.
3.  **Order & Punctuation**:
    *   If the **if/when clause** comes at the beginning, we **must use a comma** to separate the clauses.
        *   *Example*: *"If people live in a small community, they generally feel safer."*
    *   If the **if/when clause** comes after the main clause, **no comma is used**.
        *   *Example*: *"I always stop and talk to my neighbours if I see them."*
4.  **Unless**: We can use **unless** in the condition clause to mean **'if not'**.
    *   *Example*: *"A town isn't very attractive to young people unless it has good facilities."* (Equivalent to: *"if it doesn't have good facilities."*)

### 3. Vocabulary: Communities, Adjectives, & Crime

#### A. Communities: Nouns & Collocations
Master these parallel noun forms and their application:
1.  **Citizen** vs. **Resident**:
    *   *Citizen*: A legally recognised subject or national of a state.
        *   *Example*: *"Although I was born in France, I am now a Canadian **citizen**."*
    *   *Resident*: A person who resides or makes their home in a particular local place.
        *   *Example*: *"More than 60 percent of the local **residents** are retired."*
2.  **Service** vs. **Facility**:
    *   *Service*: Public systems provided by councils (such as schools, fire brigades, transport).
        *   *Example*: *"There are going to be some job losses in public **services**, particularly in schools and hospitals."*
    *   *Facility*: Physical buildings, equipment, or areas provided for a purpose (swimming pools, tennis courts).
        *   *Example*: *"My town has some good sports **facilities**, including a swimming pool and a tennis court."*
3.  **Involvement** vs. **Development**:
    *   *Involvement*: Active participation or interest in community affairs.
        *   *Example*: *"It is good to see young people's **involvement** in the local community is increasing."*
    *   *Development*: Building projects, growth, or structural expansion of a town.
        *   *Example*: *"There is a lot of **development** in my town, with new houses, shops and a new school built in the last year."*
4.  **Council** vs. **Society**:
    *   *Council*: The local government body that makes decisions for a town or region.
        *   *Example*: *"The local **council** started a recycling scheme last year."*
    *   *Society*: The community or people in general, living together under shared laws.
        *   *Example*: *"The new law will benefit all members of **society**, particularly young people."*
5.  **Cooperation** vs. **Relationship**:
    *   *Cooperation*: Working together to achieve a shared goal.
        *   *Example*: *"It would be good to have more **cooperation** between local schools and businesses."*
    *   *Relationship*: The connection or social bond between neighbours or groups.
        *   *Example*: *"I have a very good **relationship** with all my neighbours."*

#### B. Community Adjectives
*   **Urban** vs. **Rural**: Urban relates to busy cities/towns; rural relates to the quiet countryside.
    *   *Example*: *"People living in **urban** communities usually have greater job opportunities, whereas **rural** villages are often quieter."*
*   **Safe** vs. **Noisy**: Safe means free from danger; noisy means full of loud or disturbing sounds.
    *   *Example*: *"My street is very **noisy** as there is always a lot of traffic, but fortunately it is quite **safe**."*

### 1. Reading: "The New York City High Line"

#### Reading Passage: "How an old railway line became an urban garden"

**[Paragraph A]**
The High Line is now one of New York’s most attractive and popular city parks. However, its history is very unusual. It was once a railway line running above the city’s busy streets.

**[Paragraph B]**
In 1929 the railway company agreed to carry out a development programme, known as the West Side Improvement project. They built a railway above the level of the streets. This made the city much safer by getting rid of 105 level crossings. Trains began using the High Line in 1934. Factories and shops along the route had to make several changes so that items could come and go on an upper floor, without causing traffic problems at street level.

**[Paragraph C]**
As manufacturing in the city declined during the 1950s, so did train traffic. The southern section of the High Line was demolished in the 1960s, and in 1980 three carriages of frozen turkeys were the last goods ever carried by the remaining section of the High Line railway. The track had now fallen completely out of use.

**[Paragraph D]**
But then the problem was what to do with the two and a half kilometres of unused railway track. Many people thought the railway company that owned it should pull it down, but others saw some more interesting possibilities. They wanted to use the space in a way that would benefit the whole community. Discussions considered making it available for parking or for billboards, but the final decision was to transform the High Line into the city’s most unusual park.

**[Paragraph E]**
Interestingly, between 2000 and 2001 a photographer created public interest in the project when he took pictures of the disused railway line. These showed how beautiful wild flowers had started growing along the tracks and made people realise the line’s potential. A Friends of the High Line group was formed to support the redevelopment of the space. There was some political and other opposition, but eventually plans were accepted, money was raised and work could begin.

**[Paragraph F]**
Building of the first section of the park started in 2006. The first section was opened in 2009 and the second, from West 20th Street to West 30th Street, in 2011. The third section runs to the north, round the West Side Yards, which is still an active rail yard, and this was opened to the public in 2014. There are now eleven entrances to the park, five of which are accessible to people in wheelchairs.

**[Paragraph G]**
So what is it like to walk along the High Line these days? The space has been developed in a very imaginative way, with lots of different things to see and do at different points. Many New Yorkers give their time to plant and look after these, saying that it gives them the perfect opportunity after a busy day at work to enjoy spectacular views of New York and the Hudson River. There are playgrounds for children, as well as places to sit and relax or share a picnic. There are works of art to enjoy and it is often possible to watch performers. Dance parties and exercise classes are also held there. It is not surprising that the area has already been used in a number of films and TV programmes.

**[Paragraph H]**
The High Line in New York is an extraordinary example of how a city was able to transform something ugly and unused into something of true beauty, enjoyed by the whole community.

### 2. Language Development: First Conditional

#### Grammatical Rules and Uses
1.  **Usage**: We use the first conditional to speak about **possible future events** that may happen as a direct result of a specific action or situation.
2.  **Structure**: Formed using **if/unless + present simple (condition clause), will + infinitive without 'to' (main clause)**.
    *   *Modal Verbs*: Instead of *will*, we can use other modals like *can, may, might, should, must, or have to* in the main clause.
3.  **Unless**: Means **'if not'**. It introduces a negative condition.
    *   *Example*: *"The sports centre will have to close unless they raise prices."* (Equivalent to: *"if they don't raise prices."*)
4.  **Comma Rules**: Use a comma if the condition clause is written first. No comma is required if the main clause comes first.

### 3. Vocabulary: Public Services & Public Buildings

#### A. Public Services
A **public service** represents a essential utility that a national government or local municipal council provides directly for its citizens:
1.  **Education**: Primary, secondary, and higher university learning programs.
2.  **Police and fire services**: Catching criminals, maintaining safety, and responding to emergency fires.
3.  **Public transport**: Affordable municipal bus routes, subways, and train lines.
4.  **Public spaces**: Green city parks, squares, and community greens.
5.  **Public housing**: Inexpensive accommodation built for residents with lower incomes.
6.  **Health care**: Free or subsidised medical care, hospitals, and clinics.
7.  **Environmental protection**: Preventing pollution, recycling trash, and conserving nature.
