A different way to see the world

[Paragraph A] An extraordinary cyclist, Mark Beaumont, has completed a number of amazing long-distance trips on his bike. In 2008, for example, he rode for 18,296 miles around the world. Two years later he made a 13,080-mile journey through the Americas. In 2015 he cycled 6,762 miles from Cairo to Cape Town.

[Paragraph B] On that last journey Mark succeeded in breaking – by as much as 17 days – the world record for cycling across Africa. To do this, he rode 160 miles a day. He explains that it was certainly not easy to keep up this speed. There were, for example, long delays at border crossings and queues of traffic often stopped him from travelling fast. Broken roads caused problems and the weather also affected how fast he could go.

[Paragraph C] On all his journeys Mark has been interested by the landscapes he has seen. He says these often differ from what he expected. ‘When people think about Patagonia, for instance, they imagine the beautiful mountains and lakes you see in holiday brochures; but when I cycled there, I realised that large parts of it are just miles and miles of grassland. What is romantic and exciting for most holidaymakers can become very boring when you see nothing else for several days. ’

[Paragraph D] Of all the landscapes he has seen, Mark has particularly enjoyed some of the wild parts of Texas and the Deep South of the USA. ‘I remember waking before daylight. I had to remove the ice from my tent when it was still dark. Then I set out on roads with absolutely no one else around. I was in the middle of this enormous desert and all I could hear was the sound of animals calling. When I look back now, it was one of my favourite times: to be totally alone in that environment.’

[Paragraph E] His travels have also taught Mark about how differently people around the world view cycling. In Europe, North America and Australia, cycling is a sport which people do mainly to keep fit and have fun. But in many places Mark visited, a bicycle is something rather different. It is a type of transport that many people dream of getting one day. People who are used to walking everywhere wish they had enough money to buy a bike in order to get from A to B much more quickly and easily. People who see a bicycle that way often found it hard to understand why Mark wanted to cycle such long distances for no obvious reason.

[Paragraph F] But for Mark, cycling is by far the best way to see the world. ‘It’s never boring when you’re cycling. You notice everything, especially when riding alone. When you cycle, you get a wonderful view of the world. It is amazing to see how the people and cultures change from one place to the next.’ The kindness of the people Mark met was what impressed him most. ‘Even in the poorest countries, I never had to ask a second person for a place to stay.’

[Paragraph G] So how does Mark feel at the end of one of his big adventures? He says that, because the projects involve huge effort, time and costs, his main emotion is relief that he has completed the trip successfully. ‘It is not until later that you have time to think about what the journey meant, the landscapes you saw and the people you met. 5a Grammar Masterclass 05 / 21

-ing Forms & Infinitives (with/without to)

Mastering the exact usage rules for subject nouns, prepositions, purpose, and verb complementation.

1. The -ing Form
Used as nouns, after prepositions, or after specific verbs.
Rules & Triggers:
  • Sentence Subject / Noun: Acts as grammatical subject.
  • After Prepositions: after, before, in, of, for, about, instead of.
  • Certain Verbs: avoid, finish, hate, enjoy, keep, like, imagine, spend time.
• Travelling by plane is expensive.
• I feel sad after returning.
• I enjoy visiting faraway places.
2. Infinitive with to
Used to state purpose, after adjectives, or following desire/decision verbs.
Rules & Triggers:
  • Expressing Purpose: Answers why an action occurred.
  • After Adjectives: difficult, easy, hard, expensive, impossible.
  • Certain Verbs: decide, choose, hope, refuse, want, afford, prefer.
• I went to Egypt to see the pyramids.
• It is hard to sleep on a bus.
• We decided to go walking.
3. Infinitive without to
Bare infinitives used with causatives and modal verbs.
Rules & Triggers:
  • Causative/Permission: After make and let (active voice).
  • Modal Auxiliary Verbs: After can, could, should, must, might, will.
• My parents made me go camping.
• They let us walk round the site.
• We might visit local galleries.

Band 7.5+ Task 1 Model Report

The graph shows changes in the cost of fuel in the United Kingdom over the decade 1999–2009. It indicates the cost in pence of a litre of unleaded petrol compared to a litre of diesel, as well as the tax component. We can see from the data that prices fluctuated considerably over the period but generally showed an upward trend.

There was a peak in 2008, when unleaded petrol cost a high of 120 pence per litre and diesel cost approximately 135 pence per litre. The lowest price was at the beginning of the period, in 1999, when both fuels cost around 65 pence a litre.

Two things are consistent over the period. One is that unleaded petrol is always either slightly cheaper than or costs the same as diesel. The other is that tax on a litre of fuel accounts for at least half of the price. For example, without tax, the 65-pence fuel in 1999 would only have cost 10 pence.

Analyze the nature of visual degradation and match paragraphs to specific academic statements.

The problem of visual pollution

[Paragraph A] Visual pollution means that people cannot enjoy what they see. There are two types of visual pollution in the modern world: one is when visibility is limited by haze; another is visual untidiness, when buildings and signs spoil the view.

[Paragraph B] Air pollution from cars and factories reduces visibility. It looks like a brown haze over cities, but it also affects the countryside. Scientists measured the visibility at a United States national park and found that on a clear day, it is possible to see for 320 kilometres. On a hazy day, that distance falls to 48 kilometres.

[Paragraph C] Transport and industry are both major man-made causes of air pollution. But there are also natural sources of haze – smoke from forest fires, for instance. The pollution that creates haze can travel thousands of kilometres. In Southeast Asia, haze from enormous forest fires cost billions of dollars in health care. These fires have also stopped many tourists from visiting the area in the last decade. Fires in Sumatra and Borneo affected not only Indonesia, but also Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand. Developers started many of these fires – often illegally – because they wanted to use the land for building or farming. It is possible that some of the fires will continue to burn for years.

[Paragraph D] Scientists can measure haze, but people have different opinions about other forms of visual pollution. Wind turbines, billboards, power lines, mobile phone towers, even modern buildings can all cause different feelings. To the businessman, a billboard in a good location may be beautiful. But to the traveller who would like to see the hills or the pretty village behind that billboard, it is visual pollution.

5b Vocabulary 17 / 21

Weather Disasters & Climate Change Lexicon

Essential terminology for environmental essays, listening sections, and academic speaking.

Severe Weather Phenomena

Hurricane: Violent, destructive winds occurring in tropical summers.
Drought: Long, prolonged period with little or no rainfall.
Flood: Overflow of rivers/water submerging surrounding land.
Heat Wave: Extended period of abnormally high temperatures.
Thunder & Lightning: Electrical summer storms lighting up the sky.
Blizzard: Severe, freezing winter storm with heavy snowfall.
Fog: Thick cloud near the ground making driving hazardous.

Climate Change Collocations

1. Global warming: The long-term heating of Earth's climate system.
2. Polar ice cap: High-latitude region covered in ice sheets.
3. Forest fire: Uncontrolled wildfire destroying woodlands.
4. Fossil fuels: Natural carbon deposits (coal, oil, gas) burned for power.
5. Greenhouse effect: Trapping of the sun's warmth in lower atmosphere.
Accurate Collocations:
✓ Severe / extreme weather (Not *weak weather*)
✓ Heavy / strong rain (Not *hard rain*)
✓ Freezing / thick fog · High / low temperatures

The graph shows how serious people in China, India, Germany and the USA thought four environmental issues were between 2000 and 2010. Overall, attitudes towards water shortages, air pollution, climate change and automobile emissions fluctuated considerably, but people were less worried about each of the issues in 2010 than in previous years.

In general, water shortages caused more anxiety than any other issue, with 70 percent of the population rating it as ‘very serious’ in 2009. Concern about air pollution grew until 2006 before a sharp fall in 2007.

📖 Reading Strategies
  • Matching Information: Scan paragraphs for specific evidence, examples, costs, and reactions (questions are out of order).
  • Paraphrasing Awareness: Look for conceptual equivalents (e.g. "miles of grassland" ➔ "place where he felt bored").
📚 Grammatical Precision
  • -ing Forms: Used as subjects, after prepositions, and following verbs like enjoy, avoid, keep.
  • Infinitives: With to for purpose/adjectives/decisions; bare infinitives after make/let & modals.
  • Prepositions of Trend: rise by 5 (margin) vs increase to 25 (final level).
📝 Lexical Range
  • Travel & Transport: Compound nouns (flight attendant, round trip, city break) & phrasal verbs (set off, get around, check in).
  • Environmental Science: Weather disasters, climate collocations (greenhouse effect, endangered species, fossil fuels).
