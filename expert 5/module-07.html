Bournville: an unusual community

Para AVillages, towns and cities usually grow slowly over a long period of time. However, in 19th-century Britain some places developed in a very different way. This was thanks to some very unusual men.

Para BOne of the most remarkable of these was George Cadbury. He and his brother Richard had a chocolate factory in the industrial city of Birmingham. In 1879 they decided to find a new site for it and so left the city centre for a location about seven kilometres to the south. They took this decision because they thought the countryside was a healthier environment than a big city. They wanted their staff to work in a pleasant green place with fresh air. They chose a pretty location next to a little river called the Bourn.

Para CThe Cadburys treated workers much better than most industrialists did at that time. They paid them good salaries and provided pensions and a full medical service. However, they wanted to do more than just provide good working conditions. In 1890 George Cadbury bought a large area of land around the factory and starting creating, at his own expense, a whole village for the factory employees. He called the village Bournville. By 1900, the village already had 314 small houses. These were traditional in design but with large gardens and modern rooms.

Para D In 1900 the Cadburys set up a trust whose purpose was to transform the village from just a collection of houses to a community. They built shops, churches, parks, sports facilities and schools so that the people who lived in Bournville had everything they could want. In order to make it look like a traditional English village, which typically has a green open space at its centre, the Trust built the public buildings around a village green. In 1906 the Trust started a housing association called Bournville Tenants Limited. This was run by the people who lived in the village, which meant they could control their own affairs. This organisation planned new houses, chose tenants and dealt with repairs to the buildings.

Para EThe brothers were very keen on health and fitness and in 1920 they bought a large area of land next to the village, where they constructed football and hockey pitches, a running track, an open-air swimming pool and a fishing lake. This area also contained a large pavilion which had both changing rooms and a large hall used for balls and formal dinners. All the sports facilities were free for Cadbury staff and their families.

Para FSince then Bournville has continued to expand, with strong periods of growth in the 1950s and then again in the 1980s. At the start of the 21st century Bournville went through a £25-million redevelopment programme. The Trust pulled down all the flats from the 1950s because they wanted to build modern houses and apartments. In 2009 residents moved into the first of these modern homes, which have top-quality environmental standards and use much less energy than traditional houses.

Para GBournville is, of course, very different today. Although the chocolate factory is still there, many of the 25,000 people who live there now work elsewhere. The number of homes in the area has grown to almost 8,000 and these vary in size from one-bedroom flats to five-bedroom houses. Bournville is now very much part of the city of Birmingham rather than a country village, but it still has good facilities and plenty of green space. And it still considers itself a distinct community.

Grammar Focus 7a · Rules

The Zero Conditional

Universal Facts, General Habits, Comma Rules & 'Unless'

Core Formula
if / when + present simple, present simple
1. Usage:

Used for events that are always or generally true, or regular natural consequences.
"If people live in a small community, they generally feel safer."

2. 'Unless' (= 'if not'):

Introduces a negative condition without requiring double negative verbs.
"Communities don't work well unless they have a central point."

3. Clause Order & Comma Punctuation:
Condition First (Needs Comma):
"If you buy a house in the country, it usually comes with a garden."
Main Clause First (No Comma):
"Crime increases if there is high unemployment."

Zero Conditional: Verbs & Synthesis

Present Simple Conjugations & Complete Sentence Building

Ex B: Present Simple Forms

1. Cities (work) well if they (offer) facilities.
💡 Plural subject Cities and pronoun they take base forms.
2. If you (live) in a city, you (have) limited space.
💡 Second person you takes base present simple.
3. When a house (come) with a garden, people (enjoy) nature.
💡 Singular a house takes comes (-s); plural people takes enjoy.
4. Travelling (take up) time when you (live) in a city.
💡 Gerund subject Travelling is singular -> takes up (-s).

Prompt: I / not carry / much money / unless / have to
→ I don't carry much money unless I have to.

→ When I see my neighbours, I stop for a chat.

→ If I am part of a community, it makes me feel safer.
Academic Vocab 7a · Nouns & Collocations

Communities: Nouns & Collocations

Differentiating Parallel Terms & Collocation Drill

Key Noun Distinctions:
• Citizen vs. Resident: Citizen = national legal status; Resident = local inhabitant.
• Service vs. Facility: Service = public system; Facility = physical amenity/building.
• Involvement vs. Development: Involvement = participation; Development = urban construction.
• Council vs. Society: Council = local governing body; Society = broad community of people.

Interactive Collocation Drill

1. Over 60% of local are retired.
💡 residents: Inhabitants living in a local district.
2. My town has top sports , like Olympic pools.
💡 facilities: Physical buildings and athletic courts.
3. We need more from youth.
💡 involvement: Active personal participation.
4. Local approved the park budget.
💡 council: The elected municipal administrative body.

Reading · The NYC High Line 7b · Passage + Tasks

How an old railway line became an urban garden

Para AThe High Line is now one of New York’s most attractive and popular city parks. However, its history is very unusual. It was once a railway line running above the city’s busy streets.

Para B In 1929 the railway company agreed to carry out a development programme, known as the West Side Improvement project. They built a railway above the level of the streets. This made the city much safer by getting rid of 105 level crossings. Trains began using the High Line in 1934. Factories and shops along the route had to make several changes so that items could come and go on an upper floor, without causing traffic problems at street level.

Para C As manufacturing in the city declined during the 1950s, so did train traffic. The southern section of the High Line was demolished in the 1960s, and in 1980 three carriages of frozen turkeys were the last goods ever carried by the remaining section of the High Line railway. The track had now fallen completely out of use.

Para DBut then the problem was what to do with the two and a half kilometres of unused railway track. Many people thought the railway company that owned it should pull it down, but others saw some more interesting possibilities. They wanted to use the space in a way that would benefit the whole community. Discussions considered making it available for parking or for billboards, but the final decision was to transform the High Line into the city’s most unusual park.

Para EInterestingly, between 2000 and 2001 a photographer created public interest when he took pictures of the disused railway line. These showed how beautiful wild flowers had started growing along the tracks and made people realise the line’s potential. A Friends of the High Line group was formed to support the redevelopment of the space. There was some political and other opposition, but eventually plans were accepted, money was raised and work could begin.

Para FBuilding of the first section of the park started in 2006. The first section was opened in 2009 and the second, from West 20th Street to West 30th Street, in 2011. The third section runs to the north, round the West Side Yards, which is still an active rail yard, and this was opened to the public in 2014. There are now eleven entrances to the park, five of which are accessible to people in wheelchairs.

Para G So what is it like to walk along the High Line these days? The space has been developed in a very imaginative way, with lots of different things to see and do at different points. Many New Yorkers give their time to plant and look after these (gardening), saying that it gives them the perfect opportunity after a busy day at work to enjoy spectacular views of New York and the Hudson River. There are playgrounds for children, as well as places to sit and relax or share a picnic (open air eating). There are works of art to enjoy and it is often possible to watch performers. Dance parties and exercise classes (fitness) are also held there. It is not surprising that the area has already been used in a number of films and TV programmes.

Para HThe High Line in New York is an extraordinary example of how a city was able to transform something ugly and unused into something of true beauty, enjoyed by the whole community.

Grammar Focus 7b · Rules

The First Conditional

Future Possibilities, Modal Substitutions & Unless

Core Formula
if / unless + present simple, will + bare infinitive
1. Future Possibilities:

Describes real future conditions and their probable outcomes.
"If they cut the crime rate, more people will move to the area."

2. Modal Substitutions:

can, may, might, should, must can replace will to express probability or advice.
"If we get planning permission, we can build a new leisure centre."

Zero vs. First Conditional:
Zero Conditional (General Truth):
"If crime rises, people feel unsafe."
First Conditional (Specific Future):
"If the council cuts the budget tomorrow, the clinic will close."

Error Correction 7b · Exam Mistakes & Rewriting

First Conditional: Corrections & Unless

Correcting Exam Errors & Transforming Sentences with 'Unless'

Ex D: Error Correction

❌ "Where do you go if you will have free time?"
✔ Where will you go if you have free time?
❌ "If you will get home early, what will you do?"
✔ If you get home early, what will you do?
❌ "If you won't study, what will happen?"
✔ If you don't study, what will happen?

Ex E: Rewriting with 'Unless'

Orig: We won't open unless we have money. (if)
→ We won't open if we don't have money.
Orig: Museums will close if they don't get visitors. (unless)
→ Museums will close unless they get visitors.
Orig: If he doesn't stop making noise, I'll complain. (unless)
→ Unless he stops making noise, I'll complain.

Task 1 Model: Health Centre Satisfaction

Comparative Sentence Patterns & Structural Breakdown

1. Intro: The table presents information on how happy men and women are with various features of the health centre in Helby, giving percentage figures across several areas.
2. Overview: Levels of satisfaction are similar for parking and waiting areas, but considerable differences exist in ratings for lifts and maps and signs.

• "For men it is X, whereas for women it is Y"
