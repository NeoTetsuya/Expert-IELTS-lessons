Explains the optical physics of why digital cameras produce mysterious visual distortions.
Paragraph B Focus: Distance & Perception

The biggest cause of photographic artefacts is the contrast between how the ‘depth of field’ works in modern cameras and in the human eye. This is because, although it is now possible to take excellent pictures of things far away, the quality of photos of objects that are very near is less accurate. Light causes these misrepresentations. The level of light never changes when humans stare at objects. However, this is not what happens with digital cameras. When a camera points at a light, the light becomes stronger and stronger. Orbs, which are perfect circles of light, are the most common photographic artefact and they are often the result of light reflecting off water or insects in the air near the camera.

Paragraph C Focus: Shutter Timing & Mists

(1) It is easy to see why humans see differently from digital cameras. (2) However, digital cameras are also significantly different from the old film cameras. (3) In many ways, digital cameras give less reliable results. For example, modern functions allow digital cameras to scan the image more than once over a period of less than a second, or to automatically select how long the lens is open. Generally this produces better photos, but if the camera moves during the photo, it may record multiple images, creating the appearance of ghostly forms. Ghostly mists often appear when the camera picks up the photographer’s own breath on a cold night when the lens is open for longer. This never happened with film cameras.

1. Topic Sentence Location (Ex 4a):
Answer: Sentence 2 — Starts with contrast marker "However," introducing the true subject.
2. Repeated Keyword Topic (Ex 4b):
Answer: Time — The lexical chain centers on exposure duration and timing mechanisms.
3. Best Heading for Para C (Ex 4c):
Answer: Heading 1 — Inaccurate photos caused by advanced timing methods.
1. Present Simple (Routines, Facts & Permanent States)

Used for scientific facts, universal truths, scheduled timetables, and habitual routines.

👉 "Most people pay a monthly fee for their mobile."
👉 "Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius."
2. Present Continuous (In-Progress & Evolving Trends)

Used for actions happening at this moment, temporary situations, or emerging societal trends.

👉 "Times are changing fast, and more people are using smartphones."
👉 "We are offering this package at a discounted rate this week."

🔤 3rd Person Singular Spelling Rules

Standard (+s) like → likes, speak → speaks
-ch, -sh, -ss, -x, -o (+es) watch → watches, go → goes
Consonant + Y (-ies) study → studies, try → tries

⏰ Signal Words & Time Markers

• Simple: always, usually, rarely, every Monday, twice a year
• Continuous: currently, at the moment, right now, increasingly
1. Stative Nature (Non-Continuous)

State verbs describe permanent conditions, emotional states, mental perception, or possession. They are never used in continuous tenses in their stative sense.

2. Core State Verb Categories
• Thoughts: agree, believe, know, understand, think, remember
• Feelings: like, love, hate, prefer, want, need
• Senses: feel, hear, see, taste, smell, sound, appear
• Possession: belong to, own, possess, include, contain

✍️ Overview Golden Rules

  • Macro Summary Only: Never include specific raw percentages (e.g. 75%, 65%) in the overview sentence.
  • Structural Contrast: Use complex signposts like "While [Trend A], [Trend B]" to contrast starting reasons against ending reasons.

Task 1 Prompt: The graph below shows the amount of income from cinema ticket and DVD sales in the USA and internationally from 2001 to 2010. Summarise by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant. (Write at least 150 words).

The chart gives an overview of the changes in cinema and DVD sales in the United States and the rest of the world over a ten-year period from 2001 to 2010.

From the chart, we can tell that overall, DVD sales have been higher than cinema visits, and the international market has become significantly more important than the US market over the period.

At the beginning of the period, just under half of the US income came from cinema sales compared to DVDs, at $10 billion and $20 billion, respectively. Despite a slight rise in DVD sales around 2005, they finished in more or less the same position, at around $18 billion and $11 billion by 2010.

However, there were much bigger changes in international sales, with sales of DVDs rising from $11 billion to $22 billion by 2010. International cinema sales also experienced rapid growth, finishing as the largest market segment worth over $32 billion. This was more than twice as much as domestic sales. This clearly shows that the international market became progressively more important to the film industry between 2001 and 2010.

  • Macro Overview: Captures both product (DVD vs Cinema) and geographical (US vs International) shifts.
  • Comparative Phrasing: "just under half", "more than twice as much as".
